> Antarctica shall be used for peaceful purposes only. There shall be prohibited, inter
alia, any measures of a military nature, such as the establishment of military bases and
fortifications, the carrying out of military maneuvers, as well as the testing of any type of
weapons.
>
> &mdash; <cite> Article 1 of the Antarctic Treaty</cite>


In 1959, the [Antarctic Treaty](https://www.ats.aq/e/antarctictreaty.html) was signed by 12 countries designating all land and ice shelves south of 60° South latitude as a place for scientific research and where any sort of military activity or weapons testing is prohibited under international law. The original signatories were Argentina, Australia, Belgium, Chile, France, Japan, New Zealand, Norway, South Africa, the USSR, the United Kingdom, and the US. Many more countries have joined the treaty since its ratification in 1961. There are now 58 countries to accede to the treaty, the latest of which was Venezuela in 1999.

Acceding to the treaty allows a nation to participate in research activities in the continent either by establishing a scientific station there or by sending representatives on a scientific expedition. There are several facilities (research stations, depots, shelters, campsites, airbases) already established at various regions in the Antarctic. Although there are specific countries operating each of these facilities, there is often an exchange of personnel ("observers") across parties and a facility must always be open to inspection by any personnel dispatched by any party. In addition, all parties must be informed of any expedition being done within the Antarctic.

### Major Powers in Modern Times

{{< figure src="/images/maps/antarctic_facilities.jpeg" width="900" >}}

Today's major political powers have long established facilities in the Antarctic since the 1950s. 
For instance, the Soviet Union built Mirny station during the 1st Soviet Antarctic Expedition in February 1956. This was followed by Vostok (one of the few stations deep inland) in 1957. More stations were built up to the dissolution of the Soviet Union. The most modern among them, called Progress, was built in 1989.

But the most notable, being located 90°S (geographic South Pole), is the Amundsen-Scott station built by the US Navy in November 1956. It has undergone several reconstructions and expansions over the years due to structural challenges on the constantly shifting ice.
 
China entered the treaty in 1983 and established its first research station, Great Wall (Chang Cheng), in the Fildes Peninsula a year after. It also operates the second closest station to the South Pole, named Kunlun, built in 2009.
